How to prepare for a baseball tryout

To prepare for a baseball tryout, build up your throwing and conditioning in the weeks before, practice the exact things coaches will evaluate, show up early with the right gear, and compete with effort and a short memory on every rep.

By the TX Torque Coaching Staff · Updated October 22, 2026

A tryout is a short window. Coaches might see your player for an hour or two and make a decision. The good news is that most of what they look for can be prepared. Here is what we look at when we evaluate players, and how to get ready.

What coaches look for

  • Arm: how the player throws, accuracy and arm strength for his age.
  • Hitting: quality of swing and contact, not just results.
  • Fielding: footwork, glove work and getting the ball out quickly.
  • Speed and movement: running and general athleticism.
  • Effort and attitude: hustle between drills, body language after a mistake, how the player treats teammates.
  • Coachability: does the player listen and try an adjustment right away?

The last three are fully in the player's control, and they often decide close calls.

Four to six weeks before

  1. Build up the arm gradually. Do not go from no throwing to max effort on tryout day. Increase distance and volume step by step.
  2. Get in shape for the format. Tryouts include sprints and a lot of standing up and down. Add short sprints and general conditioning.
  3. Practice the evaluation. Ground balls with a throw to first, fly balls, swings in a cage, and if he pitches, a short bullpen. Practice them the way they will be run.
  4. Fix one thing, not ten. Ask a coach for the single biggest improvement and focus on that.

The week before

  • Keep training, but lower the volume the last two or three days so the body is fresh.
  • Check the gear: glove broken in, cleats or turf shoes that fit, bat that meets the league's rules, helmet if required.
  • Confirm the time, location and age group, and whether there is a registration to complete.

The night before and the morning of

  • Sleep early. Eat a normal meal, nothing new.
  • Pack water and a snack.
  • Arrive early so there is time to check in and warm up without rushing.

Tryout day checklist

  • Glove, bat, helmet and batting gloves if used
  • Cleats or turf shoes, plus sneakers
  • Athletic clothes, hat
  • Water bottle and snack
  • Catcher's gear for catchers

How to stand out on the day

  • Hustle everywhere. Run on and off the field and between stations. Coaches notice.
  • Be loud in a good way. Communicate on defense and introduce yourself to coaches.
  • Short memory. Everyone misses a ground ball or swings through a pitch. What happens on the next rep is what coaches remember.
  • Listen and adjust. If a coach gives a cue, try it immediately. That shows coachability.
  • Play your game. Do not try to do something new on tryout day. Show what you practiced.

For parents

Your job on tryout day is simple: get your player there early, rested and fed, then let the coaches coach. Encourage effort, not results. Whatever the outcome, ask the coaches what your player should work on next. That answer is worth more than the roster spot.

Position by position

Pitchers: warm up fully before your bullpen, and focus on throwing strikes with your fastball. Coaches care more about command and a clean delivery than one hard pitch.

Catchers: bring your gear, show quick feet and a quick transfer on throws, and be loud. Blocking and receiving get noticed.

Infielders: stay low, use your feet to get in front of the ball and make accurate throws. A good throw on target beats a hard throw that pulls the first baseman off the bag.

Outfielders: show good first steps and routes, and hit the cutoff on throws. Range and decision making matter as much as arm strength.

Hitters: you may only get a handful of swings. Take a good, aggressive approach and focus on hard contact rather than trying to hit a home run.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Showing up without throwing at all in the weeks before.
  • Brand new cleats or a brand new glove on tryout day.
  • Walking between stations or standing with hands on knees.
  • Arguing with a call or showing frustration after a mistake.
  • Parents coaching from the fence during the tryout.

Questions we hear before tryouts

Should my player try out for an older age group? Usually not. Players get evaluated best and develop best against peers their own age.

What if my player is new to the sport? Coaches look at athleticism, effort and coachability, not just polish. A newer player who hustles and listens can still stand out.

Do I need to bring anything besides gear? Have any registration done ahead of time and bring a way to contact you in case coaches want to follow up.

After the tryout

If your player makes it, great, the work starts now. If not, use the feedback, keep training and come back stronger. Many players make a team the second time they try out.
